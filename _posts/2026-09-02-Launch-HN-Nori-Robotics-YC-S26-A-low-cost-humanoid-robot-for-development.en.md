Imagine this: You wake up in the morning and casually say to your robot, "Could you tidy up the house today?" A moment later, the robot is busily moving around, picking up items scattered on the floor and putting them in their proper places. Until now, this has been a dream-like scenario seen only in science fiction movies. Typically, such humanoid robots have cost hundreds of thousands of dollars or were extremely rare units found only in research laboratories.

However, the price tags on these robots have recently begun to change dramatically. A startup in San Francisco, USA, has introduced a humanoid robot in the $1,000 price range, firing the starting gun for the mass adoption of robots.

### Why is this important?

Until now, the humanoid robot market has developed around expensive, high-end equipment costing hundreds of thousands of dollars for factory workers [Source 13]. While the technology was amazing, it remained far removed from the daily lives of average people. But for robots to truly enter our lives, "accessibility"—meaning a lower price—is the most critical factor.

The arrival of the Nori A3 is significant not just as a new product launch, but because it has created a new ecosystem where anyone can bring a robot into their home and participate directly in development and learning [Source 12, Source 13]. As more people own robots, the possibility of robots replacing us in solving the small inconveniences we encounter in our daily lives will increase exponentially [Source 10].

### Simply put: Why do robots need to become 'cheaper'?

Let’s use an analogy. Imagine training a smart dog. If one trainer works with only one dog, the dog can only learn the trainer's methods. But what if 1,000 people were training their own dogs in diverse environments and sharing the results? The training methods would be much more varied, and the dogs would become smart much faster by learning from each other's experiences.

The same applies to robots. The biggest hurdle in the current robot industry is the 'data bottleneck' [Source 1, Source 10]. For a robot to function in a complex and unpredictable place like a regular home, it needs data on a massive variety of situations. But because the robots themselves were too expensive to be widely adopted, there was no way to obtain the necessary data [Source 1, Source 13].

This is precisely why Nori Robotics has lowered the price of the robot to a disruptive level of $1,688 (approximately $2,200) [Source 3, Source 14]. Their strategy is to distribute more robots to homes around the world and collect diverse daily data that users obtain while training their robots [Source 10]. Once this data is gathered in one place, an era of 'collaborative learning' will begin, where all Nori robots can become smarter simultaneously [Source 1].

### Current status: What can the Nori A3 do?

The newly unveiled 'Nori A3' is a robot that moves on wheels and has two arms [Source 12, Source 13]. Its two 7-degree-of-freedom arms can each lift 1.5kg, enabling basic household assistance. It is also equipped with Lidar (a sensor that measures surroundings with lasers) capable of detecting up to 12 meters, along with four 720p cameras, to precisely grasp its surroundings [Source 8].

Users can intuitively train and manage the robot through an app called 'Nori Lab' [Source 3, Source 8]. The most interesting feature is the 'Skills Marketplace' [Source 8]. If someone develops and shares a 'technique for wiping the dining table,' other users can receive and apply this skill to their own robot with a single button. Robot capabilities are being shared in real-time. The actual launch is scheduled for this autumn [Source 3, Source 8, Source 14].

Of course, the limitations are still clear. The current model is targeted at researchers and developers, so further technical progress is needed before it becomes the 'perfect butler' we expect [Source 1]. However, the fact that it recorded over $350,000 in sales within just six weeks of launch clearly shows how much the public has been waiting for such robots [Source 2, Source 10].

### What will happen in the future?

Antonio Sitong Li, CEO of Nori Robotics, emphasizes that this robot will be more than just hardware; it will become a massive data collection platform [Source 13, Source 15]. It remains to be seen what kind of life data users will generate with their robots and what amazing skills they will upload to the marketplace.

In the not-too-distant future, we might see a world where we personally teach our robots, "Fold the laundry this way," and that data levels up the capabilities of robots around the world. Robot research has begun to step proudly out of tightly closed laboratories and into our living rooms.
