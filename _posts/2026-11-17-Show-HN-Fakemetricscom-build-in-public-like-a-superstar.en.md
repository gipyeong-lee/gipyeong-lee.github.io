Imagine turning on your smartphone in the morning and scrolling through social media, only to see numerous developers and founders posting glamorous payment platform dashboard screenshots announcing, "The app I made as a side project this week has surpassed $50,000 a month!" It is a moment that naturally makes you think, "Should I quit my job and build an app too?" But what if those flashy growth charts are fake, intricately crafted with the click of a button?

Recently, a humorous website posted on the global developer community Hacker News became a major topic of discussion [Source 1]. With the emergence of a tool that instantly creates realistic fake success stories, the pros and cons of the "Build in Public" culture—which has taken Silicon Valley and the global startup scene by storm—have returned to the spotlight.

---

## Why This Matters

For the past few years, "Build in Public" has been considered a core cheat code for solo founders (indie hackers) and early-stage startups to achieve success. Instead of building a flawless product in secret and launching it out of the blue, the strategy involves transparently sharing the bumpy development process, failures, and revenue milestones to gather a fandom and early customers.

However, as this transparency has become a standardized marketing commodity, critics note that it is mutating from a tool for demonstrating "real value" into a technique for packaging oneself as "successful" [Source 12]. The rise of tools like Fakemetrics.com playfully pokes fun at the flaws in this trend while posing a sharp question about how we should view the countless "startup success stories" we encounter on social media.

---

## Simplified: Fake Dashboards and the Theater of Transparency

Put simply, if **Building in Public is a restaurant with an "open kitchen,"** founders gain customer trust by showing the cooking process. Customers develop high expectations for the food as they watch the chef sweat to make homemade sauce.

This analogy makes it easy to understand. The original intent was to share the cooking process honestly and receive feedback, but an increasing number of people are now "hanging up polished Michelin 3-star plaque photos on their kitchen glass without actually cooking anything."

**Fakemetrics.com**, created by developer Tigran, is a toy-like website that lets anyone print out this "plaque photo" in a single second [Source 1]. While experimenting with a system that precisely clones UI using Claude Opus—the latest artificial intelligence model—he completed a tool that remotely whips up Stripe user growth screenshots that look like massive hits despite generating zero actual revenue [Source 1]. Tigran himself reflected, "I tried this experiment because a friend of mine tried to pose as fake-successful a few months ago," adding, "Is it useful? Probably not. But is it fun? Yes!" [Source 1].

This tool ironically reveals why people obsess over flashy metrics and why they are easily seduced by others' success stories.

---

## Current Situation: How Far Has It Come, and What Are the Issues?

Today, "Build in Public" has grown beyond a mere development culture into a massive ecosystem.

- **Trends by the Numbers**: On TikTok, content related to `@build_in_public` has garnered over 700,000 views across just 40 major videos, drawing mainstream interest [Source 3]. The relevant community on Twitter (X) has gathered over 33,000 members, and the indie hacker community platform `indiehackers.com` boasts over 100,000 subscribers [Source 17].
- **Positive Effects**: Founders have clear reasons for sharing their development process. Building a startup is different from writing a book; it requires constant feedback to find Product-Market Fit (PMF) [Source 5]. Transparent sharing serves as the fastest feedback channel and a powerful customer acquisition tool based on the truth that "people buy from people they know" [Source 5].
- **The Dark Side (Plateaus and Fatigue)**: However, the side effects are significant. Engaging in Build in Public activities on social media platforms like Twitter often leads to severe growth plateaus around the 10,000-follower mark due to algorithm saturation within existing networks [Source 20].
- **Performance Art and Competitor Exposure**: Some critics point out that public building today has degenerated into a sort of performance art, where more time is spent packaging "perfect and cool-looking update news" than actually building the product [Source 12]. Furthermore, bitter post-mortems note that sharing source code and strategies too transparently ends up serving free R&D information to competitors on a platter [Source 13]. In reality, when 3,000 people "like" your launch announcement only to find out you had just 4 actual sales in the first week, admitting that truth is so agonizing that many choose silence or inflate their metrics [Source 21].

---

## What Lies Ahead?

As technology and platforms evolve, the era of "showy transparency" is slowly hitting its limits.

In the near future, strategies that rely merely on emotional or exaggerated screenshots to catch the public eye are likely to lose traction. Readers and consumers are growing fatigued by countless fake metrics and flashy AI-generated marketing. Instead, authentic communication—sharing the unvarnished, raw reality of failures alongside product perfection that solves real problems—will survive.

The popularity of bizarre yet humorous tools like Fakemetrics.com teaches us an important lesson: visible metrics aren't everything, and true innovation is forged not by numbers in a screenshot, but through the heavy process of quietly writing code and listening to user voices.
