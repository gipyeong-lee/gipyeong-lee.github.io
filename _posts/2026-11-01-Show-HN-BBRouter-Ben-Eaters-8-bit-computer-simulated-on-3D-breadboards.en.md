Imagine this: What if the inside of the smartphones or laptops we use every day was actually a structure densely woven with very simple 'switches'? Modern devices are so complex that it is difficult to grasp their principles at a glance, but the story changes completely when you look into the process of building the foundations of a computer from tiny pieces, one by one. A virtual simulation project that has recently become a hot topic among hardware enthusiasts has moved the process of building a computer, as if assembling Lego blocks, into a virtual space on our screens.

### Why is this important?

For most people, a computer is like a 'black box' whose interior cannot be seen directly. However, this project vividly shows that the cutting-edge technology we enjoy is not a magical phenomenon, but the result of layered logical designs. Hardware design usually requires handling physical parts directly, so even a single wiring mistake can burn out components or make error correction very difficult. The introduction of this 3D simulation technology is significant in that it has opened a new path for learners to freely experiment with and learn the core principles of hardware without the risk of damaging expensive parts.

### Easy to understand: A computer born on a breadboard

This project was inspired by the famous '8-bit computer build project' released by educational content creator Ben Eater. Ben Eater directly plugged logic gates (semiconductor devices that logically process electrical signals) into breadboards (boards that allow circuit configuration without soldering) to build a fully programmable 8-bit computer from the ground up [Source: BenEater](https://eater.net/) [Source: Build an 8-bit computer | BenEater](https://eater.net/8bit). 

In simple terms, he implemented how the 'brain' of the computer we use processes signals of '0 and 1' by manually connecting the basic electronic parts. He meticulously recorded this process in a tutorial video series, helping countless people understand electronics and computer architecture [Source: BenEater - YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCS0N5baNlQWJCUrhCEo8WlA).

The simulation that has emerged this time brings this challenging process into a virtual space. This is similar to the circuit simulation method of Tinkercad. Tinkercad also provides a very useful environment where users can connect parts like Arduino or micro:bit in a virtual environment in advance and observe how circuits react before connecting actual physical wires [Source: Bring your 3D designs to life with Circuit Assemblies using Tinkercad.](https://www.tinkercad.com/circuits).

### How far have we come?

Many users are now enjoying building Ben Eater's designs or conducting various experiments directly within their web browsers without purchasing actual hardware. Videos showing an 8-bit computer functioning normally in this simulator environment are already being actively shared online [Source: Ben Eater's 8-bit computer on simulator - watch videos online...](https://rutube.ru/video/ae00c18fb5eca0abfc2eabf274b432e0/). This serves as a practical guide for those taking their first steps into the world of complex hardware, significantly narrowing the gap between theoretical learning and actual implementation.

### What will happen in the future?

Moving forward, the method of learning complex electronic circuits in a virtual environment without physical constraints will become more widespread. As sophisticated designs can be easily shared by anyone and modified on the fly, it is expected that new dimensions of innovation will occur not only in educational settings but also in hobbyist fields. If you have ever wondered how a computer actually works, why not try designing your own computer on your screen now?
