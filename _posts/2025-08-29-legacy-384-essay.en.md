<p>
The home where we were born and raised is called the family of origin, which includes our parents and siblings. The household newly formed through marriage or independence is called the nuclear family, which centers on spouses and children.
</p>

<p>
One of the problems many couples face after marriage is the distance between their family of origin and their newly formed family. When parents intervene frequently, the couple's decision-making can be shaken, and the home, which should be the most private sanctuary, loses its sense of comfort. When parents get involved in household chores or child-rearing, it can lead to a blurred sense of boundaries regarding who is the master of the house.
</p>

<p>
I have been feeling this discomfort quite often lately. I feel uneasy when my mother-in-law visits our home. So, I keep asking myself, "Am I the problem?" This is actually the reason why I am writing this post. I want to know if my discomfort is merely due to my own sensitivity or if it is a natural phenomenon.
</p>

<p>
Family therapist Murray Bowen (1978) emphasized the concept of "<b>differentiation of self</b>." He explained that when one cannot properly detach from the family of origin, the marital relationship becomes unstable and conflicts increase. A study in Korea (Choi Jung-yoon & Kim Eun-jung, 2011) also reported that excessive enmeshment with the family of origin increases marital conflict, while maintaining an appropriate distance improves marital satisfaction. If so, the discomfort I am feeling may not be a personal weakness, but a natural signal that I need to protect my new family.
</p>

<p>
This issue is particularly prominent in Korean society. Confucian culture emphasizes honoring parents and filial piety. Economic realities, such as housing prices and child-rearing costs, also narrow the distance from parents. There is still a strong sentiment that "families must do everything together," so establishing boundaries can sometimes be perceived as selfish. Studies by Song Jung-sook (2014), Kim Young-hee (2019), and others point out that conflict intensifies when the autonomy of a couple is threatened under such cultural pressure.
</p>

<p>
As I write this, I am trying to convince myself: feeling discomfort is not wrong; rather, it is a signal that healthy boundaries are necessary. Visits from parents should be scheduled in advance, and while help should be gratefully received, important decisions must be made by the couple. Parents can be advisors, but the final decision must rest with the couple.
</p>

<p>
International research (Young & Kleist, 2010) echoes the same sentiment. It suggests that the clearer a couple defines boundaries with their family of origin, the higher their intimacy and satisfaction become.
</p>
<p>
In the end, what I want to confirm through this piece is simple: it is not because there is something wrong with me that I feel uncomfortable, but rather that my desire to protect my family is naturally surfacing. Prioritizing my new family without excluding the family of origin is the balance I hope to achieve right now. A distance that is close but not intrusive, and far but not alienating. The process of finding that distance may be the very confusion I am experiencing, and at the same time, the learning I need.
</p>
