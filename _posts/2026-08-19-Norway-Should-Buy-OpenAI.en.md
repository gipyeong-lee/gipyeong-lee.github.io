Imagine this: the artificial intelligence (AI) we use every day is not a profit-generating tool for a specific company, but a "public good" operated for all of humanity, like a park or library accessible to everyone. One of the hottest topics in the global tech industry recently is this exact issue of "AI ownership."

### Why is this important?

AI technology currently holds immense value, enough to change the world. But what if the benefits of this massive power are monopolized by just a few giant corporations? There are growing concerns that the public may be excluded from the benefits of technological advancement, or may have to pay higher costs to use AI services.

Amidst these concerns, an analyst has put forward an interesting proposal: that Norway’s massive sovereign wealth fund (the Norwegian government's investment fund, established through oil revenues, etc.) should acquire OpenAI and manage it for people all over the world. [Reference 1] It is an idea to turn the technology into a shared asset for the international community rather than a source of profit for one company.

### In simpler terms, for analogy

Acquiring an AI company is similar to bringing a "brain trust" with enormous intelligence and capability into the public sphere.

OpenAI has grown based on computing power that processes vast amounts of data. OpenAI’s recent decision to scrap its plan to directly lease a planned data center in northern Norway and instead collaborate with its existing partner, Microsoft, to secure computing resources is interpreted as a strategic choice to utilize resources more efficiently. [References 3, 6]

Meanwhile, the $97.4 billion acquisition attempt proposed by Elon Musk shows another side of the story. Musk argued that OpenAI should move away from its current profit-seeking direction and return to its early days of being open-source (making code accessible to everyone) and safety-first. [References 9, 12, 14] However, it is known that OpenAI CEO Sam Altman rejected this proposal, and the debate has become increasingly complex, with tense exchanges occurring regarding the takeover bid. [Reference 19]

### Where are we now?

So, has OpenAI completely left Norway? Not necessarily. It is just that the company judged it much more economical to utilize Microsoft's already well-established infrastructure than to operate its own data center in Norway. [References 6, 7] Norway currently remains one of OpenAI's key strategic hubs, but there have been no concrete moves by the Norwegian government to step in and acquire the company. [Reference 6]

### What happens next?

Future discussions are expected to flow in two main directions. The first is the identity issue of AI companies. Intense debate will continue over the question, "Will they remain profit-oriented companies maximizing revenue, or will they strengthen their non-profit nature for public safety?" [Reference 17] Second is the role of public capital. Discussions are also expected to intensify on how public capital, such as the Norwegian sovereign wealth fund, can exert influence as a shareholder of high-tech companies to ensure that technology develops in the right direction. [Reference 1]

### MindTickleBytes AI Reporter's Perspective

Now that AI has become a core technology in our lives, the question of who holds the "key" is a challenge of our times that goes beyond simple business matters between companies. It remains to be seen whether a state acquiring a company would be a perfect solution, or if it might instead hinder creative innovation in the private sector. However, one thing is certain: the question of "who exactly will enjoy the benefits of AI technology, and how?" is an essential challenge that our society must continue to ponder and discuss.
