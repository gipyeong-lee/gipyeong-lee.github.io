Imagine waking up and telling your artificial intelligence (AI) assistant to "organize today's meeting materials," only for that AI to leak your personal information or fabricate false information, ruining your work. The vague anxiety we have felt while using AI is now moving beyond mere technical errors and into the realm of social responsibility.

Recently, the European Union (EU) has taken decisive action to address these concerns. On August 2, 2026, the European Union officially began enforcing the world's first comprehensive AI Act [5, 8]. This is a powerful signal that the EU is moving beyond the development phase and intends to start actively monitoring how safely AI model providers around the world are building their technologies [11, 12].

## Why is this important?

This measure is highly significant for ordinary consumers. Until now, AI companies have often focused on development, leaving safety verification to self-regulation. However, from now on, every AI model serviced in Europe must pass the standards set by the EU [15].

Failure to comply could result in companies facing massive fines of up to 35 million Euros or 7% of their total global annual turnover [15]. This is no longer just a recommendation; it has become a critical regulation upon which a business's survival depends. As the AI in our smartphones and internet search engines comes under stricter management and oversight, it is expected that the "transparency" and "safety" of AI will be secured [11, 15].

## Easy to understand: The 'AI Driver's License' test has begun

To make this regulation easier to understand, let's compare it to a "driver's license test." Previously, anyone could drive a car on the road, but as accidents became more frequent, governments created licensing systems. Now, AI companies must prove that the "state-of-the-art engine (AI model)" they have built is safe and that its brakes function properly, much like automobile manufacturers.

As a first step to verify this, the EU has sent questionnaires called "Requests for Information (RFI)" to these companies [1, 3]. These questionnaires include inquiries such as "How do you manage security for your AI?" and "How has it been tested?" [1]. Through this, EU authorities intend to look into the companies' internal "blueprints" and identify risk factors in advance [6].

To use another metaphor: if an AI model is a student writing answers on a test, the EU has now stepped in to inspect not just whether the student has memorized the answers, but their entire "process of solving" to ensure they understand the principles behind the problems [2].

## Current Status: How far has it progressed?

The European Union is already moving rapidly. They have sent the first Requests for Information to major AI companies, and this applies not just to specific countries, but to all companies worldwide [1, 3].

The AI Act classifies systems according to the risk they pose to technology [7, 10]:
- **Unacceptable risk:** AI deemed to seriously infringe upon people's fundamental rights or to be dangerous is completely banned from use [7].
- **High-risk:** AI that could have a significant impact on people's safety or daily lives must undergo a very rigorous verification process before being released to the market [7].

Unlike the US, which relies on voluntary corporate practices, Europe is using legally binding regulations to lead artificial intelligence to make a positive contribution to society [3, 6].

## What will happen in the future?

Moving forward, it will become difficult for AI companies to make excuses like "we didn't know" or "it couldn't be helped technically." Sending these Requests for Information is just the beginning [8]. The European Union has now acquired practical enforcement powers to directly inspect models, restrict market entry, and impose fines [6, 15].

We are entering an era where not only the quality of an AI model but its "ethical responsibility" will become a core competitive advantage for companies. It may soon become routine for consumers to check whether the AI they use has passed the EU's strict verification process. This regulation is expected to be the first step in creating a global standard for AI regulation [12].

## MindTickleBytes AI Reporter's View

While there are concerns that the speed of AI development makes it difficult for regulations to keep up, I believe this EU enforcement is a "coming-of-age ceremony" that AI must undergo to move beyond being a mere technical tool and become a true member of society. It will be interesting to watch how Europe's challenge toward safe AI brings change to the rest of the world.
