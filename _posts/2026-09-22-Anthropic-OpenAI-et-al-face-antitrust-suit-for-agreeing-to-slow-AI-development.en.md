Imagine this: You use a cutting-edge AI service for which you pay a significant monthly subscription fee. You click the payment button with the expectation that it will be smarter tomorrow than it is today. How would you feel if you discovered that these AI service companies had gathered with their competitors and promised, "Let’s all develop a little, very slowly"?

Recently, an intriguing lawsuit was initiated in the United States against major AI companies regarding this very suspicion. It is not simply about whether the technology is good or bad; it is a battle over whether it is legally right for companies to artificially decide the 'pace of technological advancement.'

### Why It Matters

This lawsuit is directly linked to the wallets of everyday users. The subscribers who filed the lawsuit argue that the collusion of these companies is ultimately blocking qualitative improvements in services and undermining the technological value that should be returned to consumers [Source 14].

The reason we pay monthly for AI services is the expectation that the technology will evolve rapidly. If companies intentionally slow down development under the pretext of 'safety,' consumers are essentially being deprived of the technological benefits they are rightfully entitled to. This ruling is expected to serve as a significant benchmark determining what principles AI companies must uphold between 'safety' and 'market competition' when formulating their technology development strategies [Source 4, Source 14].

### The Explainer

To understand this situation easily, let's use an analogy. Suppose there are two very famous restaurants. Customers want more innovative and delicious dishes, but the restaurant owners collude, saying, "If we develop too quickly, the chefs might make mistakes and harm the customers, so let's stop developing new menus this month."

There are two points of contention here. One is 'essential consideration for safety,' and the other is the 'disappearance of competition.'

In reality, industry leaders like Dario Amodei of Anthropic, Sam Altman of OpenAI, and Elon Musk have continuously discussed that speed regulation is necessary to prevent risks if so-called 'Frontier AI' (cutting-edge AI that exceeds current technological limits) becomes powerful enough to build next-generation models itself [Source 10].

However, the plaintiffs who filed the lawsuit view this discussion not as a simple safety measure, but as **'collusion to evade competition.'** The core argument is that the act of Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and SpaceX AI agreeing to artificially match their speeds is a violation of Antitrust Law (laws regulating corporate monopolies and collusion) that hinders market competition [Source 2, Source 3, Source 7].

### Where We Stand

On September 18, 2026, four subscribers filed a lawsuit (Buist et al. v. Anthropic PBC et al.) against Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceX AI, and Google in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California [Source 3].

Companies have been raising their voices about the need to slow down development by emphasizing the potential risks that artificial intelligence could pose to society [Source 9, Source 10, Source 11]. However, this lawsuit points out that this justification has instead resulted in distorting fair competition in the market. Especially at a time when astronomical amounts of capital are being injected into AI infrastructure across the United States, it raises the possibility that the companies' arguments for 'speed regulation' are actually sophisticated strategies to protect their economic interests [Source 11, Source 14].

### What's Next

The outcome of this antitrust lawsuit could completely change the landscape of the AI industry. If the court acknowledges the collusion allegations against the companies, they will face heavy legal responsibility for acts that limit market competition under the guise of 'safety' [Source 2, Source 3].

Conversely, if the court rules in favor of the companies, the specific nature of AI technology will be acknowledged, and the act of companies autonomously regulating their speed for technological safety will be considered justifiable. Now that technology is changing at the speed of light, we are waiting for an answer in court to the fundamental question: "At whose speed should technology develop?"
