Imagine if someone had already written in detail 60 years ago about the algorithmic dominance we experience daily on our smartphones and the normalcy of conversing with Artificial Intelligence (AI). It may sound like a prophecy, but if you open the books of renowned Polish author and futurist Stanisław Lem, you will encounter exactly such records.

The writings he produced in 1964 give the impression that you are reading the latest AI papers or technical memos flooding the internet today [Source 10]. How could an author from 60 years ago envision the technological frenzy we face today so vividly?

## Why does this matter?

We are living in the 'Age of AI'. Large Language Models (LLMs, AI technology that learns from vast amounts of text to generate sentences) now write news articles, write code, and even hold conversations with us. However, amidst these changes, we often mistake technology as something that has emerged suddenly, like magic.

Lem's insights teach us an important lesson: the AI craze we are experiencing today is not a phenomenon that fell from the sky overnight. For a long time, Lem warned in philosophical and literary language about how humanity would handle technology and what shadows that technology would cast upon our society [Source 9]. Through his writings, we gain lenses to view the reality of current technology more objectively and profoundly.

## Easy to Understand: The 'AI Prophecy' from 60 Years Ago

'Summa Technologiae', a collection of non-fiction essays published by Lem in 1964, comes as a great shock to modern readers [Source 10].

Simply put, this book is like a 'blueprint' that anticipated the technical problems we would face in the future. Metaphorically speaking, Lem sharply criticized other science fiction writers of his time for merely writing stories where they had sword fights in future settings (taking past adventures and moving them into the future) [Source 13]. He posed the essential question: how will technology escape human control and change our way of life?

His other masterpiece, 'The Cyberiad', was also first published in 1964 [Source 3, Source 11]. This short story collection deals with the boundary between machines and humans, and the paradoxes that occur when technology seems perfect. It is as if he had already literary captured, 60 years ago, the absurdity we feel when we command models like ChatGPT to "do all my work," only to find the results sometimes nonsensical or meaningless [Source 2].

## Current Situation: How Far Have We Come?

Stanisław Lem's books have become classics, translated into 52 languages and sold over 40 million copies worldwide [Source 4]. Lem was not just a novelist, but a profound philosopher and futurist, and he continued his technological critique across society until his passing in 2006 [Source 8].

We are currently standing in the midst of the technology Lem predicted. The way people use smartphones or Large Language Models and ask questions like, "Will this technology really help my child's development?" or "Is this true?" aligns exactly with the critical thinking about technology that Lem emphasized throughout his life [Source 12]. Lem continuously reminded us that if humans do not reflect on themselves as technology advances, technology could slip into a 'state of loss of control' [Source 12].

## What Will Happen Next?

Technology will continue to advance, but Lem's teachings will remain valid. Lem will continue to make us question not only the level at which AI reads and summarizes papers, but how the way we use technology itself is changing the essence of humanity [Source 9].

As AI becomes smarter, we must contemplate more fiercely what 'Mediocre garbage' is hidden behind the convenience provided by technology, and what we should ask of AI [Source 2]. What Lem left us 60 years ago might be a precious legacy—not a mere technical prediction, but the 'critical perspective' that humans living with technology must never forget.

## MindTickleBytes AI Reporter's Perspective

Reading Stanisław Lem's writing is like looking at our current selves through a 60-year-old mirror. Technology evolves rapidly, but fundamental human curiosity and vague anxiety about technology are no different now than they were 60 years ago. We can either master technology or become the opposite. Lem's warnings will serve as a compass for the AI era we face today.
