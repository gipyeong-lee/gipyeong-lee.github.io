Imagine you are the captain of a massive ship and need to control its speed. If you go too fast, the engine might overheat and explode (inflation), but if you go too slow, you won't reach your destination on time (sluggish employment). The 'captain' steering this massive ship called the U.S. economy is the Federal Reserve (often referred to as the Fed).

The Fed recently announced an interest rate hike through its FOMC (Federal Open Market Committee) statement [Source 8]. What exactly is this 'interest rate' that consistently appears in economic news, and why does it shake up our ordinary lives?

## Why Does This Matter?

The Fed's decisions are not just a U.S.-specific issue because the United States is the world's largest economy. When the Fed raises interest rates, global capital tends to move toward the U.S. in pursuit of higher returns. This impacts exchange rates and, consequently, has a direct effect on inflation, stock markets, and the loan interest rates we have to pay in South Korea.

In this latest announcement, the Fed voted unanimously, 12 to 0, to raise the benchmark interest rate by 0.25 percentage points, from 3-3/4% to 4% [Source 8]. This demonstrates a strong commitment to slowing the speed of the economic ship—in other words, to curb inflation.

## Making It Easy: What Is the 'Dual Mandate'?

The work of the Fed is commonly referred to as the 'Dual Mandate' [Source 8]. These two core missions are **'maximum employment'** and **'price stability'** [Source 2].

1. **Maximum Employment:** Helping as many people as possible maintain stable jobs.
2. **Price Stability:** Preventing the value of money from falling too quickly—that is, keeping inflation from rising excessively.

Adjusting interest rates is like operating a faucet. If inflation (the water) is too hot, the Fed raises rates to tighten the faucet and cool down economic activity. Conversely, if the economy cools down too much (poor employment), it lowers rates to turn up the heat. The Fed aims to maintain an inflation rate of 2% over the long term [Source 13].

Simply put, imagine if the price of bread went up by 10% every month. If your salary stays the same but it becomes harder to afford bread, the economy would fall into chaos. In this situation, the Fed raises interest rates to discourage people from spending money, ultimately reducing demand for bread and trying to break the upward trend in prices.

## What Is the Current Situation?

According to the recent statement, the Fed made its commitment to protecting these two economic goals clear through this rate hike [Source 8]. In truth, the current situation is not easy. While there were times in the past when economic activity grew evenly and the labor market was strong [Source 6, Source 10, Source 17], several economic headwinds are currently blowing [Source 18].

The Fed carefully examines various intertwined economic indicators to determine the scope of its interest rate decisions [Source 12]. Sometimes it cuts rates to revive the economy [Source 5, Source 9], and at other times, it raises them—as it did this time—in an effort to precisely control the engine of the economic ship [Source 8].

## What Will Happen Next?

Because the economy is like a living organism, it is difficult to predict even a step ahead. However, what is certain is that the Fed is responding flexibly depending on the economic situation. The Fed has pledged to use all its tools to "support the smooth functioning of the economy and financial conditions" [Source 12].

Moving forward, it will be important to watch the Fed's statements to see how much inflation has stabilized and whether the labor market remains healthy. Since rising interest rates can increase the burden of loan interest, you should prepare for the time being by reducing excessive spending and managing your cash flow to ensure you remain prudent in your financial activities.
