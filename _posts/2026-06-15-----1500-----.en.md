## Why Is This Important?: From the Neighborhood Bakery Menu to the Tears of SMEs

"I heard the exchange rate went up, but I have no plans to travel abroad this summer anyway, so isn't this irrelevant to me?" If you thought this, you are gravely mistaken. The fact that the exchange rate is approaching 1,500 won is not limited to the shallow meaning of having to pay more money when buying a cosmetic product at a duty-free shop. In a structure like our country, which lacks the basic materials to make goods and stakes the fate of the national economy on exports and imports, the exchange rate is like the 'blood pressure' flowing through the veins of the entire economy. Just as high blood pressure slowly eats away at the health of the entire body, the phenomenon of a rising exchange rate (the decline in the value of the won and the rise in the value of the dollar) immediately triggers a spike in the prices of imported goods coming into our country, making the entire economy sick.

Let's use a very simple analogy. Think of an owner running a small, warm bakery in a neighborhood alley. To bake delicious bread, high-quality flour imported from vast lands like the US or Australia is absolutely necessary. Let's assume they import 1kg of flour worth 1 dollar in the global market. Once upon a time, when the exchange rate was 1,000 won, the owner only had to hand over a 1,000 won bill to the bank to exchange it for 1 dollar and pay the importer.

But what happens when the exchange rate jumps to 1,500 won like it is now? Simply put, the bakery owner now has to pay 1,500 won, which is 1.5 times the amount, to import the same 1-dollar flour. The amount of flour is the same, but the cost of ingredients has skyrocketed by a whopping 50%. The owner cannot run a business by emptying their own pockets and taking a loss every day. In the end, with tears in their eyes, the owner has no choice but to raise the price tags of the red bean bread and salt bread, and the wallets of us who buy and eat that bread become thinner in the blink of an eye. The 'inflation bomb' goes off, causing a chain reaction of soaring prices for almost all goods dependent on imports, from the coffee beans we drink every morning to the car gas we pump on the way home, and even the ultra-precision components that go into smartphones.

Especially for small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), which lack ample capital and have weaker fundamental strength compared to large corporations, the current high exchange rate comes not just as a change in numbers, but as a 'threat to survival' that might force them to close their doors. In reality, many domestic SMEs that engage in both exporting and importing are staggering after taking the sharp direct hit of the high exchange rate head-on [[Theqoo - "Even export companies can't smile"... SMEs hit hard by high exchange rate](https://theqoo.net/square/4236719966)]. They have to buy raw materials from overseas, but because the exchange rate bounces around by 10 or 20 won every morning, they cannot calculate what their costs will be a month from now, leaving them engulfed in extreme anxiety.

Interestingly and unfortunately, amid such a bleak situation, many SMEs are hesitant and delaying even subscribing to a financial safety net called **'Currency Hedging'** [[Theqoo - "Even export companies can't smile"... SMEs hit hard by high exchange rate](https://theqoo.net/square/4236719966)].

Hold on a second, shall we perfectly break down the term 'currency hedging', which frequently appears in economic articles but remains somewhat vague? Metaphorically speaking, you can think of currency hedging as **'car insurance that protects against exchange rate fluctuations'**. Regardless of whether the exchange rate goes crazy up or plummets down in the future, you stamp a seal with the bank in advance, making a firm promise: "When our company needs dollars next month, no matter what the market price is, let us unconditionally buy them at 1,400 won!" Why are companies hesitating right now about this exchange rate insurance, which normally should be a sturdy shield protecting the company?

The reason is that the current market direction is so treacherous that it is impossible to predict. Driven by the psychological expectations of some companies cautiously guessing that the exchange rate 'will rise further from here', a phenomenon has occurred where they are weighing whether subscribing to the insurance right now would be profitable or a loss, thereby delaying the timing of their subscription [[Theqoo - "Even export companies can't smile"... SMEs hit hard by high exchange rate](https://theqoo.net/square/4236719966)]. It's due to the uncertainty in the thick fog of not knowing how the exchange rate situation will bounce tomorrow if they get the insurance today.

Nevertheless, because the fear that 'something really bad is going to happen at this rate' cannot be suppressed, the total volume of funds subscribing to currency hedging after deep deliberation has ballooned abnormally. As of the end of May this year, the size of funds subscribed to currency hedging products reached a staggering 534.7 billion won, an enormous figure that surged by 31% compared to the same period last year [[Theqoo - "Even export companies can't smile"... SMEs hit hard by high exchange rate](https://theqoo.net/square/4236719966)]. This 31% growth rate is chilling evidence that perfectly illustrates the extreme economic dilemma company executives are experiencing as they toss and turn every night.
