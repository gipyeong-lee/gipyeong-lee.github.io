Imagine you are the owner of an international online shopping mall. Last year, paying 1,300 won per dollar was enough to source your goods from the US. One day, you wake up to find that you suddenly have to pay 1,470 won to get the exact same item. Even if you sell the same volume, your profit will plummet, or you might even suffer an unfair loss. What choice would you make in this situation? Naturally, you would try to reduce imports from the US or temporarily scale down your business.

This is exactly how foreign investors have been looking at the Korean stock market recently. If you open your stock market app, which seemed like it would rise endlessly on the back of the Artificial Intelligence (AI) craze, you will find more and more days where a bitter blue market (downward trend) greets you. It's not because AI technology has suddenly stopped advancing. It's because the two giant gears that move the economy—'interest rates' (the price of borrowing money) and 'exchange rates' (the exchange ratio between different countries' currencies)—are turning in an unusual way. Why are these numerical games happening in the US directly affecting our precious assets and Korea's representative companies? Today, we will unpack the difficult economic stories often heard in the news as easily and kindly as a conversation with a friend over a cup of coffee.

## Why It Matters

For the past year or two, the most frequently heard word around us has undoubtedly been 'AI (Artificial Intelligence).' Since the appearance of ChatGPT, massive amounts of money from all over the world have poured into AI-related companies like a waterfall. From the National Pension Service, the vital retirement fund of our citizens, to small individual monthly savings, everyone was directly or indirectly riding this massive wave of technological innovation.

However, news suddenly arrived that threw cold water on the seemingly eternal festival atmosphere. The biggest reason that halted the global stock market rally was the emergence of the 'possibility of US interest rate hikes' [Source: Will the US Really Raise Rates? "Semiconductor Earnings and Exchange Rate Variables... Must Be Cautious"](https://v.daum.net/v/20260608203713979).

What does this have to do with our daily lives? If the US, the world's number one economic power, raises interest rates, Korea also takes that hit directly. In fact, looking at economic indicators reveals a surprising fact: Korea has gained the painful dishonor of being the country with the highest interest rate increase among major global nations in 2026 [Source: Interest Rates Pulled Up by Exchange Rates Following Semiconductors... Will It Be a Variable for the Stock Market?](https://www.hankyung.com/article/202606123195i).

Rising interest rates mean 'the cost of borrowing money becomes more expensive.' The interest burden on people who bought houses by stretching their finances to the limit increases, and companies trying to expand their businesses by developing new technologies become afraid to cross the bank's threshold. Consumers tighten their wallets, and companies reduce investment. Naturally, the 'surplus money' that would enter the stock market dries up. In other words, a single interest rate number decided in Washington D.C. has crossed the Pacific to directly shave off our grocery prices, loan interest, and the returns on our stock accounts—a very serious situation.

## The Explainer

In economic news, the term 'Federal Reserve (Fed, the US central bank, like the Bank of Korea)' often appears. The global economy fluctuates depending on whether they regularly hold meetings to raise or lower interest rates. Why is that?

Think of it this way: money (capital) in the world has a property of constantly moving toward a giant 'magnet' that is both safe and pays high interest. The largest and strongest bank in the world is the 'US.' If the US raises its interest rates (increasing the pulling power of the magnet), saying, "We'll give you much more interest if you deposit money in our bank!", investment funds spread across the world, including Korea, will pack their bags and rush to the US.

According to expert analysis, the Fed's interest rate decision does not strike the Korean stock market directly but rather triggers a series of domino effects. It is a shockwave that first raises the interest rate of US Treasury bonds (certificates issued when the state borrows money), then widens the interest rate gap between Korea and the US, and ultimately causes the value of the dollar relative to the won (won/dollar exchange rate) to soar, causing foreign investors to coldly leave the Korean market (deterioration of supply and demand) [Source: Analysis of US Fed Interest Rate Decision: Impact on Korean Stock Market and Response](https://standardecon.co.kr/entry/미국-연준Fed-금리-결정-분석-한국-증시에-미치는-영향-및-대응).

So, why does the US bother to raise interest rates and make the whole world nervous? To grab the leash of frighteningly rising 'prices (inflation).' Simply put, current US energy prices and core inflation (the real core inflation excluding items with large temporary fluctuations like food) are not falling as expected and are showing signs of becoming entrenched. To suppress this stubborn inflation, they are still considering the interest rate hike card, which leads to concerns about further increases in long-term market interest rates.

The problem is that if this high-interest rate situation continues, fatal side effects occur. Even representative Korean semiconductor companies that need to build cutting-edge factories and borrow astronomical amounts of money for technical research, such as Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, feel serious pressure and burden in raising funds [Source: U.S. Inflation and Interest Rate Variables Emerge as Burden Factors for Korean Stock Market](https://tradersunion.com/ko/news/financial-news/show/2190010-us-inflation-interest-rate-korea-stock-market/).

## Where We Stand

So, what does the report card for the Korean economy look like right now? The situation is harsher than expected. As foreigners' dollars withdraw like an ebbing tide, the value of our currency (won) has plummeted. Recently, the won-dollar exchange rate surpassed 1,470 won, which economic entities consider a psychological limit. This has soared to the highest and most fearsome level since the giant economic disaster of the 2008 global financial crisis [Source: [Jung Won-seok's Macro Review: Seoul Apartment Price Anxiety That Pulled Up Treasury Bond Interest Rates]...](https://v.daum.net/v/20251128121429915?x_trkm=t). Furthermore, with the interest rate of domestic 3-year government bonds—bonds issued when the state borrows money—approaching 4%, the flow of money in the market has essentially frozen solid. [Source: "Forgotten Due to Semiconductors, but Something Big is Coming Soon"... Bottom-Fishing K-Defense with 'Solid Performance'...](https://www.mk.co.kr/news/stock/12069376).

The ones who moved the fastest in these harsh conditions were, of course, foreign investors. Their calculators are extremely cold. Although the KOSPI index once showed a general upward trend and seemed to enjoy a brief boom this year, foreign investors net sold (sold much more than they bought) astronomical amounts of stocks worth 130 trillion won during this surge period alone and suddenly left the Korean market [Source: Liquidity Trapped in Interest Rates and Exchange Rates... What Foreigners Who Net Sold 130 Trillion Won are Really After...](https://magazine.hankyung.com/business/article/202606034573b). To put it simply, 130 trillion won is a huge amount of money equivalent to about one-fifth of Korea's annual national budget. No matter how much Korean stock prices rose, they packed their bags without hesitation because they would suffer significant foreign exchange losses (losses due to exchange rate fluctuations) when converting back to dollars due to the exchange rate.

However, there is no need to despair too much. Our economy is not just collapsing helplessly. Although costs for importing energy and overall prices rose significantly due to external shocks like the war in the Middle East, the demand for Korea's specialty, 'semiconductors,' has exploded thanks to the massive historical trend of expanding global investment in Artificial Intelligence (AI). The Bank of Korea, while nervous about oil prices and high interest rates, announced that it has raised this year's economic growth forecast to 2.6%, much higher than the original forecast of 2.1%, as semiconductor exports continue to show a strong trend that greatly exceeds expectations [Source: Smiling at Semiconductors, Nervous at Oil Prices... "Long-term High Oil Prices, AI Investment Adjustment, etc. as Variables"](https://www.msn.com/ko-kr/money/경제/반도체에-웃고-유가에-긴장-고유가-장기화-ai투자-조정-등-변수/ar-AA24fMTG).

To use an analogy, in the middle of a dark sea where heavy rain and wind (high interest rates) are blowing and waves (high exchange rates) are fearsomely surging, an incredibly powerful modern motor engine called 'Korean Semiconductors' is silently pushing a giant ship forward, preventing the economy from sinking.

## What's Next

So, how long will this motor of the semiconductor boom, which is firmly supporting our economy, keep spinning vigorously? Fortunately, many economic experts are offering positive outlooks that this engine will not easily shut down for the time being. According to an in-depth analysis by the Bank of Korea, the massive semiconductor cycle we are currently experiencing is expected to be maintained for a long period similar to the 'dot-com bubble (a period of soaring IT tech stocks)' that occurred when the internet was first distributed globally. It is expected that this hot favorable trend will continue at least until the end of next year [Source: Bank of Korea: "Semiconductor Cycle Until the End of Next Year... Period Similar to Dot-com Bubble"](https://www.wowtv.co.kr/NewsCenter/News/Read?articleId=AKR20251127132700002). This is because a massive amount of high-performance semiconductors is essential, like a human brain, to properly build and smartly train large AI systems like ChatGPT. Until the world builds most of its AI infrastructure (foundation facilities), Korea's semiconductor factories will keep their lights on 24 hours a day without rest.

Ultimately, the most important key variable and challenge depends on when the 'exchange rate,' which is jumping like a roller coaster, will calmly find stability. Stock market researchers point to 'won strength (exchange rate decline, i.e., the value of our currency rising)' as the most powerful relief pitcher to lead the Korean stock market upward again. If the won-dollar exchange rate breaks significantly against market concerns and expectations in the coming March or April and turns into a clear downward trend, investors will gain strong confidence that the value of Korean money relative to the dollar will increase. Experts believe that if this happens, the strong selling pressure from foreigners that has been weighing down the Korean stock market will melt away, and instead, buying pressure from domestic financial investment firms will act strongly, making it highly likely that the stalled stock market will regain vitality once again [Source: Policy, Next Variable to Lead the Stock Market After Semiconductors... "Won Strength Will Emerge"](https://m.cartech.nate.com/content/2165701).

## AI's Take

In conclusion, the current stock market is a fierce battlefield where rosy future prospects brought by AI and the harsh interest and exchange rate figures of the real economy grab each other's collars and engage in a tight tug-of-war every day. Just because the stock account on your smartphone is colored blue doesn't mean the world has ended. While the strong shield of semiconductors, boasting the world's best technology, supports our economy, it is a time when wise wisdom is needed to carefully watch the news for when the soaring exchange rates and US inflation indicators will finally turn.

**MindTickleBytes AI Reporter's Take:** Technological innovation races ahead endlessly without brakes, but ultimately, it is supported by the cold numbers of capitalism—'interest rates' and 'exchange rates.' No matter how strong the sails and motor engines creating cutting-edge AI chips we have, if the weather of the wide macroeconomic sea (US monetary policy) is harsh, the ship cannot help but shake. Now is the time to calmly observe the macroscopic flow and study the economy to see when these two giant gears of the economy will smoothly interlock and turn again, rather than being swept away by blind technological praise or premature fear.
