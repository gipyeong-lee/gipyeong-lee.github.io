Imagine this: You are excitedly planning your first summer vacation in a while. You imagine the beautiful scenery of Europe and click on a travel agency site, but you let out a deep sigh at the price tag on the screen. The exchange rate has soared, and when you add the rising cost of airplane fuel (fuel surcharge) to the airfare, it has far exceeded your planned budget. Ultimately, many travelers are choosing to change their destinations this summer, saying, "Let's go somewhere closer instead of Europe this time."

## Why It Matters

The recent sustained high exchange rates and high oil prices are going beyond simple economic indicators to change our daily leisure lives and travel culture. Instead of simply giving up on overseas travel, travelers are making "short-distance cost-effective travel"—where they can fully enjoy the fun of travel while minimizing costs—the new standard [Source 10]. This shift in consumer trends is serving as an opportunity to fundamentally change the profit structures and marketing strategies of the travel industry in the long term [Source 2].

## The Explainer

How has the economic situation changed our way of traveling? To understand this easily, let's compare travel to "putting together a puzzle."

Originally, putting together a large, flashy puzzle called "long-distance travel" was a great pleasure of vacation. However, the price (exchange rate, fuel costs) to obtain those puzzle pieces has recently become too expensive. So, people are turning their eyes toward "short-distance puzzles" that are a bit smaller and less burdensome to put together [Source 1, Source 9].

In response, the travel industry has introduced a strategy called "3-No products" to ease the cost burden on consumers [Source 5]. These are products that have reasonably lowered prices by removing shopping, optional tours, and tips, and containing only the most essential itinerary [Source 5]. It is similar to how we remove unnecessary filters and effects when using photo apps, leaving only the clearest and most important parts. In addition, airlines are focusing on marketing that induces repeat visits, such as providing discount coupons targeting the "N-th travel" demand of those who have visited a place before [Source 7, Source 15].

## Where We Stand

In reality, demand for short-distance travel is noticeably increasing in the market, led by low-cost carriers [Source 1]. Japan, in particular, has established itself as a representative destination for this type of short-distance travel due to its relatively low exchange rate [Source 9]. Domestic travel is also gaining consumer trust, centered on products with reasonable costs and transparent itineraries [Source 2].

However, there are not only positive signals. As the price of gasoline, the core of vehicle transportation costs, exceeds 2,000 won per liter, a phenomenon is appearing where even domestic travel demand is shrinking [Source 8]. Also, although the total number of travelers has increased, the actual profitability of the aviation industry is not as high as expected due to the structural cost burden of high exchange rates and high oil prices [Source 12, Source 15].

## What's Next

For the time being, this trend of "cost-effective, short-distance travel" is expected to continue. Experts analyze that this phenomenon is not just a temporary response, but a mid- to long-term survival strategy for the travel industry to gain consumer trust amidst the high-inflation and high-exchange-rate environment [Source 2]. In the future, travelers will more carefully examine transparent pricing policies and practical products, and airlines and travel agencies are expected to continue a constant tightrope walk to catch the two rabbits of cost reduction and customer satisfaction.

## MindTickleBytes' AI Reporter Perspective

The economic environment is shifting the form of travel from "physical distance" to "practicality." It is expected that the wise choices of travelers who try to consume strategically instead of just closing their wallets will, as a result, bring positive changes that scrape away the bubbles in the travel industry and improve its constitution.
