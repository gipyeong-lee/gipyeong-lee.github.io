Imagine flying over Earth in an airplane and looking down. You might think, "Ah, there are so many trees on Earth," seeing the endless expanse of lush green carpet-like forests. But did you know that hidden within the gaps of that green carpet, or among the sparsely scattered trees, are countless lifelines supporting Earth's ecosystem?

With the recent dramatic advancements in satellite technology, we have moved beyond simply identifying where forests are and how extensive they are. We can now meticulously examine the health of those forests and understand the roles played by the 'trees outside the forest' that we've previously overlooked.

## Why is this important?

For a long time, we've understood forests simply as 'areas densely populated with trees.' However, forests are not monolithic. There are forests that regenerate naturally without human intervention, and there are also artificially managed forests.

Accurately assessing forest health is the first step towards addressing climate change and preserving biodiversity. In the past, only areas with densely packed trees were considered forests. However, in reality, billions of trees existing outside of dense forests play a crucial role in regulating Earth's carbon emissions and conserving the environment. [A study on the trees that global forest mapping data had overlooked](https://news.worldrainforests.com/2025/07/14/global-forest-mapping-missed-396-million-hectares-of-trees/) clearly illustrates how narrowly we have defined the scope of forests.

## Easy to understand: 'Tree Filtering' Technology

Satellite mapping technology is very similar to the 'filter' function in a photo app. Just as we can highlight a person or blur the background to emphasize them when taking a photo, satellite data also has criteria to select vegetation above a certain height.

Typically, satellite data defines vegetation over 5 meters in height as 'trees' and quantifies this to create [forest maps](https://earthenginepartners.appspot.com/). This makes not only dense forests but also the scattered trees around them clearly visible on the map. Simply put, it's like observing a 'vegetation map' showing tree distribution superimposed transparently onto a 'topographical map' that displays the overall terrain, allowing for a three-dimensional view. Using [tree cover maps of tropical regions](https://landcarbonlab.org/data/tropical-tree-cover/), one can more finely read the ecosystems inside and outside dense forests.

## Current Situation: Records of Forests

What is the current state of Earth's forests? Analyzing recent satellite data reveals several interesting and important facts.

1.  **Russia's Vast Forests:** The country with the largest forest area in the world is Russia, spanning over 800 million hectares. [Statistics from Our World in Data](https://ourworldindata.org/forest-area) show the significant proportion of Russia's forests within the Earth's total forest area.
2.  **Slowing Forest Loss:** Unfortunately, forest area has steadily decreased over the past decades. In the case of naturally regenerating forests, 324 million hectares were lost between 1990 and 2025. However, there is also good news. According to the [2025 Global Forest Resources Assessment](https://openknowledge.fao.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/2dee6e93-1988-4659-aa89-30dd20b43b15/content/FRA-2025/key-findings.html), the annual net forest loss rate has slowed by nearly half, from 13.8 million hectares in the 1990s to about 6.97 million hectares recently.
3.  **Signs of Nature's Recovery:** The preciousness of forests is proven through past recovery cases. For example, Costa Rica, which had forests covering half its territory in 1950, saw this shrink to 25% by 1995 due to agricultural expansion and other factors. [The subsequent signals of forest recovery](https://news.mongabay.com/2026/04/listening-to-forests-reveals-signs-of-recovery-beyond-tree-cover/) clearly indicate how carefully we must observe and preserve nature's resilience.

## What will happen in the future?

In the future, technology will evolve beyond simply measuring forest cover loss to real-time analysis of [how forests are changing (forest trends)](https://www.conservationgateway.org/collections/climate/natural-climate-solutions-resources/forests/interactive-world-forest-map-tree-cover-change-global-data/).

Specifically, [cutting-edge mapping techniques](https://earthenginepartners.appspot.com/) that track 'stand-replacement disturbance' – the phenomenon where a forest is completely transformed into a different land type – in real-time will provide policymakers with strong scientific grounds for deciding which areas require priority protection.
