Imagine this: You have song lyrics you've spent months writing, staying up late to perfect. Then one day, someone has an AI perform a simple button press, and text almost identical to your lyrics appears on the screen in seconds. It’s the moment you start asking, "Can we really say the AI created this on its own?" Recently, giants of the music industry have drawn their swords against an AI company over exactly this issue.

World-renowned music publishers Sony Music Publishing and Warner Chappell Music have filed a massive, multi-billion dollar lawsuit against AI company Anthropic. [Source: Sony Music and Warner Chappell's Anthropic lawsuit (Source 3, Source 9)] This case is being counted as one of the most brazen and massive instances of intellectual property infringement in the music industry. [Source: Music publishers' Anthropic lawsuit (Source 1, Source 6)]

### Why does this matter?

This lawsuit goes beyond a mere dispute between companies; it is sounding an alarm about how the AI in our daily lives handles creative works. If AI provides services based on training itself on someone's creative output without permission, human artists may find themselves without a place to stand. [Source: Sony and Warner's Anthropic copyright lawsuit (Source 2, Source 11)]

The music publishers argue that Anthropic’s AI model, "Claude," is a direct threat to the ecosystem of human creators. [Source: Sony and Warner's Anthropic copyright lawsuit (Source 2)] If it is proven that the AI services we use every day are infringing upon someone's rights, the entire AI industry is highly likely to hit the massive wall of copyright law.

### Simply put, this is the situation

To help you understand, let's use an analogy. "AI training" is similar to quickly reading millions of books in a massive library and summarizing their content. But what if that library isn't checking out books legitimately, but is instead filled with pirated copies?

According to the music publishers' claims, Anthropic used similar methods, utilizing digital archives such as "Library Genesis" or "Pirate Library Mirror" to obtain copyrighted materials. [Source: Sony and Warner's Anthropic copyright lawsuit (Source 2)] In other words, instead of asking for permission from the rightful owners, they took materials from a warehouse someone else had illegally collected to train the AI.

The AI trained in this way is said to have gained the ability to go beyond simply imitating musical styles, reaching a point where it can actually generate copies almost identical to protected lyrics. [Source: Sony and Warner's Anthropic copyright lawsuit (Source 2)] It is similar to a situation where a chef doesn't just learn a famous recipe to create a similar flavor, but actually copies the recipe itself to reproduce the exact dish.

### Current situation and the heart of the controversy

The music industry is currently reacting very sensitively to copyright issues. It is easy to understand the situation if you recall the aggressive response the music industry showed when services like Napster or LimeWire appeared. [Source: Anthropic-related lawsuit news (Source 8)]

The music publishers are strongly criticizing Anthropic and its founders for engaging in illegal activities related to copyright infringement. [Source: Sony and Warner's Anthropic copyright lawsuit (Source 11)] Of course, Anthropic has not made an immediate statement regarding this lawsuit and is currently unreachable. [Source: Anthropic-related lawsuit news (Source 6, Source 8)]

### Future outlook

This lawsuit appears set to turn into a fierce, multi-billion dollar legal battle. [Source: Anthropic-related lawsuit news (Source 3)] Depending on the court's ruling, the way copyrighted materials are used in the AI model training process could fundamentally change. It is also highly likely that AI companies will need to introduce stricter copyright verification procedures when collecting training data in the future. Creators will now watch more closely how their works become "food" for AI, and for the AI industry, securing legal legitimacy will become as important as technological innovation.

Technological progress always advances in conflict with existing order. However, we must reconsider the way creators' efforts are being consumed indiscriminately in the name of "data." I hope this lawsuit becomes an important starting point for creating healthier rules where technology and art can coexist.
