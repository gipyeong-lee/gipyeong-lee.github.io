Imagine this: your business is a massive success. Customers are flooding in, and you have more money in the safe than ever before. Yet, strangely, the value of your currency is not recognized outside the shop, so you have to pay much more than usual every time you want to buy something. The current economic situation in South Korea is exactly like this.

South Korea achieved record-breaking monthly exports of $87.8 billion in May 2026 based on customs clearance, with semiconductor exports alone accounting for $37.2 billion [Source 5]. The trade surplus for this year has already exceeded $100 billion [Source 3]. Economically, we are "raking in the money," so why is the value of our currency, the won, stuck at a high exchange rate in the 1,500-won range?

### Why is the exchange rate important?
Exchange rates are not just numbers; they are directly linked to the "cost of living" in our daily lives. If a high exchange rate of over 1,500 won persists, the prices of raw materials and energy imported from overseas rise, pushing up domestic prices. Even if exports are doing well, if the value of the won is low, the actual economic feeling perceived by the public inevitably drops. President Lee Jae-myung also recently commented that the won/dollar exchange rate in the mid-1,500s is somewhat excessive compared to the current fundamental strength of Korea's economy (the country's underlying economic health) [Source 8].

### An easy explanation
It is easy to understand if you think of it this way: **The exchange rate is the flow of dollars that have entered through the "export pipeline" into the "won pond."**

In the past, when exports were good and a lot of dollars flowed through the pipeline, they were naturally converted into won, and the water level of the pond was appropriately adjusted. In other words, the formula "if the exchange rate rises, exports increase and the economy improves" worked [Source 6].

However, today, the pipeline (exports) is operating thicker and more powerfully than ever, but these dollars are not flowing directly into the won pond; they are leaking elsewhere. It is not a problem of the ability to earn dollars, but a "problem of capital flow" where the earned dollars fail to return after being converted into won [Source 4].

### What is the current situation?
Currently, our economy looks flashy on the outside, with the KOSPI breaking all-time highs, but it is struggling in the foreign exchange market [Source 3]. On June 4, 2026, the won/dollar exchange rate rose to 1,531 won during intraday trading, the highest level since the 2009 global financial crisis [Source 5, Source 7]. While export performance in core industries like semiconductors is dazzling, a "discrepancy" where this success does not lead to exchange rate stability has been continuing for several months [Source 7].

### What will happen in the future?
Economic authorities are also taking action. The government began efforts to reduce shocks in the foreign exchange market by opening the era of 24-hour won/dollar trading starting on the 6th [Source 1].

What we should focus on in the future is not the export scorecard itself, but whether the "flow of funds"—that is, the path through which these export proceeds circulate in the financial market—is improved. As the economic fundamentals are solid, if the flow of funds normalizes, there is a possibility that the excessive high exchange rate phenomenon will gradually ease.

### AI Opinion
The export indicator, which is the "health check result" of the national economy, is at an all-time high, but the exchange rate, which corresponds to "blood pressure," is at a crisis level. This may be a sign that there are cracks in various parts of our economy's pipeline where dollars are stalling or leaking elsewhere. It is now a point in time when "how efficiently you put the money you earned back into the market" is more important than "how much more you earn."
