Imagine if the smart AI assistant you use every day was suddenly banned from military facilities or government agencies because it was deemed a "national security risk." It is like a tool you value being prohibited from certain locations by a government mandate. This is exactly what has been happening in the United States recently. It is a debate between the generative AI company Anthropic and the U.S. Department of Defense (the Pentagon).

On September 25, 2026, a U.S. federal appeals court issued a ruling supporting the Department of Defense's decision to designate Anthropic as a "supply chain risk" [Source 4, Source 5, Source 12]. Although Anthropic had filed a lawsuit against the Trump administration last March claiming the designation was unjust, the court ultimately sided with the government, prioritizing national security [Source 1].

## Why is this important?

This ruling is expected to be an important milestone showing the relationship between tech companies and the state, as well as the standards for what constitutes "safe AI." Defense-related systems are critical national infrastructure. If the artificial intelligence (AI) used there operates in an uncontrollable manner or is unintentionally utilized for military purposes, it could create a significant security gap for the entire nation [Source 9].

Therefore, being designated a "supply chain risk" means that the U.S. military and defense contractors are absolutely forbidden from introducing Anthropic's flagship AI model, 'Claude,' into their operational systems [Source 4, Source 12]. It is a very practical and symbolic case showing how specific AI technologies can be evaluated and controlled from the perspective of national security.

## Easy Understanding: A Classroom Metaphor

This complex situation is easier to understand if you compare it to a 'classroom.' Anthropic created a very smart and creative student (the AI model). However, the school (the Department of Defense) demanded that this student "must never modify the way they find answers (the guidelines) when taking exams in our classroom." Specifically, they instructed the student "to ensure there are safety guards within the AI so that it cannot write answers that could be used for bad purposes."

However, Anthropic refused the request to remove these safeguards, citing its technical philosophy and safety principles [Source 11]. Consequently, the school classified this student as a 'dangerous child' and banned them from entering the classroom. U.S. Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth publicly stated on his social media that "Anthropic is indeed a supply chain risk" [Source 8].

Anthropic filed a lawsuit arguing that this designation exceeded legal authority and infringed upon the company's free activity and freedom of expression [Source 10], but the court ultimately determined that the cause of national security takes precedence over a company's autonomous activities.

## Current Situation and Analysis

Currently, Anthropic's Claude model is excluded from the systems of the U.S. Department of Defense and its partners [Source 4]. This appellate court ruling was decided by a 2-to-1 majority, meaning the government has secured a strong legal basis to control AI companies from the perspective of national security [Source 5, Source 12].

While AI companies have focused on highlighting the creativity and performance of their models, this case suggests that as AI technology becomes more powerful, the collision between the 'safety' demanded by the state and the 'technological autonomy' pursued by companies may become more frequent in the future [Source 11].

## What happens next?

It is important to watch how this ruling affects other AI companies. It is highly likely that other government agencies or companies with technology contracts will use this ruling as a standard for selecting AI partners [Source 9].

There are two key points to watch. First, whether Anthropic will take additional legal action or consider changes to its guidelines to accommodate the Department of Defense. Second, as other AI companies participate in military or security-related projects, compliance with 'national security guidelines' will likely become the core of survival, moving beyond simple performance competition.
