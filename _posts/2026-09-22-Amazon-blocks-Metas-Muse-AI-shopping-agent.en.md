Imagine this: On a busy morning, you say to your AI assistant on your smartphone, "Order the detergent and coffee I put in my cart yesterday." The AI then connects to the shopping mall and completes the purchase for you. Wouldn't that be convenient? Recently, Meta released an AI shopping assistant called 'Muse' to turn this dream into reality. However, just 12 days after its release, this assistant was turned away by Amazon, the world's largest shopping mall. [Source 11] Why on earth did this happen?

## Why is this important?

This incident is not merely a tussle between two tech giants. It is the prelude to 'Agentic Commerce' (an era where AI shops for people autonomously), which will determine how we use AI in the future and who controls our shopping data. [Source 8, Source 15]

Platforms like Amazon hold all the data on what we search for and what we buy within their malls. This data is the core of Amazon's $68.6 billion advertising business. [Source 15] However, when Meta's AI connects to Amazon on our behalf, Amazon loses the opportunity to communicate directly with customers and analyze their data. From the perspective of a platform company, it could see AI roaming around its backyard as an act of "crossing the line." [Source 6, Source 16]

## Simple analogy: A visit from a 'Secretary'

To put it simply:

**Situation A (The Past):** You personally go to a department store (Amazon) to choose and purchase items. The department store understands your purchasing habits and sends you coupons later.

**Situation B (The Present - Agent):** Your 'personal secretary' (Muse AI) enters the department store for you. From the department store's perspective, it cannot know what you, the actual customer, see when you visit or which ads you react to. It is just an 'unknown secretary' buying items.

Amazon has viewed this 'secretary' as a sort of intruder. Amazon claims that Muse accessed the site in an unauthorized manner and raises concerns about 'Credential Capture,' where the AI seemingly collects user account information without consent. [Source 5, Source 11, Source 14] Meta, on the other hand, is expressing its frustration, stating, "Muse never views sensitive personal information such as user passwords or payment details." [Source 10, Source 11]

## Where do we stand now?

Meta's Muse AI is currently blocked from the Amazon website. [Source 12, Source 17] On September 20, Amazon officially blocked access to Muse, which had been out for 12 days. [Source 11]

In short, this situation is a head-on collision between the 'walls of a platform' and the 'mobility of AI.' Amazon wants to control all activities within its walls, while Meta wants to send AI into those walls so that users can shop freely from anywhere. [Source 8, Source 16]

## What will happen next?

This incident is just the beginning. More AI agents will be acting on our behalf in various areas such as shopping, travel, and reservations in the future.

From a user's perspective, the dilemma between convenience and security is bound to deepen. Should we trust an 'AI assistant' that manages our data safely, or do we prefer the secure environment provided directly by a 'platform'? Through this block, Amazon has once again highlighted the importance of 'platform control.' It is expected that the criteria for 'digital entry passes' (authorization) required for AI agents to connect to other platforms will become even stricter. [Source 14, Source 15]
