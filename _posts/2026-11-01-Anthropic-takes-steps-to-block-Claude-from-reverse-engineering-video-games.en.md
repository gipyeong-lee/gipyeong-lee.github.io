Imagine this: You miss a classic game you enjoyed as a child, but the original source code is nowhere to be found, and the game company is long gone. What if you could show the game file to an AI, and it would redraw the game's 'blueprint' and create a program that functions exactly the same way?

Recently, such experiments have become possible and have sparked a major controversy among developers. But as this incredible ability raised unexpected copyright issues, AI developer Anthropic took emergency action to put the brakes on it. What exactly happened?

## Why does this matter?

AI is now evolving beyond a simple conversational partner into an 'agent' (a program that autonomously performs tasks) capable of analyzing complex software and writing code. While this means a massive boost in productivity for developers, it is also a 'double-edged sword' that can lead to unauthorized infringement of copyrights on existing software or creative works.

If anyone could easily clone a game that someone else spent years painstakingly building, creators might lose the motivation to make new content. Anthropic's move is an important event that goes beyond simply blocking a feature; it is about establishing ethical standards for how far we should allow technology to go in the AI era.

## Simple explanation: Analyzing AI's 'cooking'

Have you heard the term 'reverse-engineering'? It refers to the process of taking apart a finished machine to draw a blueprint of how it works.

To put it simply, it’s like tasting a restaurant's secret sauce, analyzing the ingredients, and finding the recipe to recreate the exact flavor. Until now, this process required extremely high-level expertise and a long time. However, in a recent experiment, when a researcher connected an AI tool called Claude Code to a disassembler (a tool that turns a program’s machine code into a readable language), the AI successfully analyzed the game, reproduced the source code, and created an identical game [Source 9](https://xakep.ru/2026/02/19/claude-reverse/).

In short, it’s as if the AI tasted a 'finished dish' called a game, guessed all the ingredients and cooking sequences inside it, and recreated the exact same dish. Anthropic judged that this capability could be misused to clone specific games without permission.

## Current situation

Anthropic recently updated its Claude AI model, 'Opus 5.5.' This update plays a role in 'steering' the AI to refuse requests when asked to reproduce specific video games [Source 2](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/programming/anthropic-appears-to-have-ordered-ai-tool-claude-to-stop-remaking-video-games).

In other words, they have installed a 'safety net' to maintain the AI's powerful coding capabilities while ensuring it does not cross the line into cloning someone else's work. The Claude services currently available to the public are designed to provide safe and accurate work support, and this measure can be seen as the result of further strengthening those safety guidelines [Source 3](https://claude.com/).

## What happens next?

Technological advancement will not stop. However, from now on, AI developers will equip their models with more sophisticated 'ethical navigation.'

What we need to pay attention to is whether AI will remain a tool that provides 'creative inspiration' or whether it will degenerate into a 'tool for cloning existing creative works.' New rules that can respect copyrights while maximizing AI productivity will continue to be created. As users, we should enjoy the convenience AI provides while keeping an eye on whether a healthy AI ecosystem is established where the rights of creators are protected.

## MindTickleBytes' AI Reporter Perspective

Technology is always ahead of our imagination. While the AI's ability to analyze code has the potential to revolutionize the development environment, it is time to think about what the minimum etiquette we must keep is when crossing that threshold. Anthropic's move is significant in that it marks the beginning of AI learning not just 'what it can do,' but 'what it should not do.'
